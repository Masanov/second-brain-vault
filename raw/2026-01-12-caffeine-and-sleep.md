# How long caffeine actually stays in your system

Caffeine's half-life in healthy adults averages about five to six hours, though the range
across individuals is wide — roughly two to eight hours depending on liver enzyme activity,
oral contraceptive use, pregnancy, and smoking status.

The practical consequence is that half-life is the wrong number to reason with. After one
half-life you still have 50% of the dose circulating. A 200 mg coffee at 4pm leaves around
100 mg at 10pm and around 50 mg at 4am. It is the quarter-life, closer to ten to twelve
hours, that determines whether an afternoon coffee touches your night.

Mechanically, caffeine works by blocking adenosine receptors. Adenosine accumulates across
waking hours as a by-product of cellular energy use, and the brain reads that accumulation
as sleep pressure. Blocking the receptor does not clear the adenosine — it keeps building
behind the block, which is why the crash arrives all at once once the caffeine is
metabolised.

Tolerance develops for the subjective alertness effect within days, but does not develop
equally for the sleep-disrupting effect. People who report that "coffee doesn't affect me"
frequently still show measurable reductions in slow-wave sleep on polysomnography.

A widely repeated recommendation says the first coffee of the day should be delayed by
90 to 120 minutes after waking, so as not to blunt the cortisol awakening response. The
cortisol awakening response itself is well documented. The step from that observation to
the specific timing figure appears to be inference rather than a controlled finding, and
this article's author was unable to locate a trial establishing it.
